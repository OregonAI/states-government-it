Executive Summary
The Washington State Office of Equity is excited to partner with you and advance a future where everyone can
participate in the decisions and policies that impact them. We advance systems change so everyone has full
access to the opportunities, power, and resources they need to flourish and achieve their full potential (RCW
43.06D.020). Our team analyzes the outcomes and human impact of our decision-making to drive
accountability within the Washington State government.
On January 30th, 2024, Governor Inslee issued Executive Order 24-01 – ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE (EO 24-01).
“To ensure algorithmic justice as it relates to digital equity, by September 2024, the Office of Equity will
oversee the development, implementation, and ongoing support of an overarching framework for
accountability in the fair, ethical, and equitable use of generative AI by the state.
    a) The accountability framework will be created in partnership with WaTech, DES, and the Workforce
       Training and Education Coordinating Board to de-silo and remove barriers for agencies seeking to
       test and implement generative AI tools.
    b) The accountability framework must be comprised of a transparent, systematic, and continuous
       monitoring of outcomes and impacts on communities and must include the intentional and
       consistent engagement of communities.
    c) The accountability framework should be informed by subject-matter experts from across the
       Enterprise, community members, Tribal governments, impacted front-line state employees, and
       academic and industry stakeholders whose purpose will be to co-create an assessment process
       and performance measures.”

What is Generative AI?
Section 1 of EO 24-01 defines Generative AI (GenAI) as: “A technology that can create content, including text,
images, audio, or video when prompted by a user. GenAI systems learn patterns and relationships from large
amounts of data, which enables systems to generate new content that may be similar, but not identical, to
the underlying training data.”
As stated in the State of Washington Generative Artificial Intelligence Report: “Because of its unique ability to
generate content on its own without relying on being fed pre-existing data, GenAI is commonly used in the
application of creating content such as image generation, storytelling, and document writing.”
Below are some examples of how GenAI might be used:

    •   Image Generation: Tools can create detailed, photorealistic images based on text prompts,
        enabling artists, designers, and marketers to visualize concepts quickly.
    •   Content Creation: Platforms are used by content creators and marketers to draft articles, social
        media posts, and even entire books, offering creative support and speeding up content production.
    •   Code Generation: Tools assist developers by generating code snippets based on comments or
        partial code, reducing development time and improving coding efficiency.
    •   Music Composition: AI-driven platforms generate custom music tracks for videos, games, and
        podcasts, tailored to the user’s specific needs and preferences.
    •   Video Production: Generative AI is being used to produce and edit videos, creating realistic scenes
        or deepfakes with minimal human input.

Governance
Governance should be built on existing structures within the state enterprise or agency. We recommend that
you implement these guidelines through your existing governance structures as much as possible.

Components of Effective Governance:
    •    Clear goals: Define clear goals and objectives to achieve intended outcomes. Ensure that the use of
         GenAI provides benefits our communities, and mitigates disparities, risks, and harm.
    •    Roles, responsibilities, and monitoring: Define clear roles, responsibilities, and delegation of
         authority to ensure effective operations, timely corrections, and sustainable, transparent
         accountability. Accountability cannot be sustained without monitoring performance and impact.
         Make use of the existing systems and structures but identify any gaps that need to be addressed.

    •    Values: Demonstrate a commitment to the guiding principles (page 5) to foster public trust in the
         responsible use of GenAI. Intentionally build for access and positive impact on underserved and
         vulnerable communities.
    •    Community involvement: Include diverse perspectives from a variety of community voices
         throughout the usage of GenAI, when applicable. Consider the needs of our communities and
         whether the use of GenAI serves them.
    •    Transparency: Promote transparency by enabling open access to information on the design,
         operation, and limitations of the GenAI system as much as possible. Establish a way for community
         members to give feedback and submit complaints on specific uses of GenAI enabled applications
         that directly impact them.

Equity
The state of Washington is committed to accuracy, reliability, and ethical outcomes when adopting GenAI.
Washington State and its agencies should engage and support historically vulnerable communities and serve
all those who reside, work, or do business in Washington in a transparent, engaged, and equitable way.

Equity Guidelines
•   Emphasize human involvement and human impacts: Involve human reviewers in high-risk use cases.
    Identify biases, analyze impacts, and check for unintended consequences at all stages. The agency is
    responsible for reporting on results, communications, and decision-making.
•   Accessibility and plain language: This is a requirement of Executive Order 23-03 for all public
    information. It emphasizes the importance of upstream communication in creating downstream impact.
    Content creators are required to address accommodation needs and language barriers, ensuring
    transparency and effective communication to make GenAI systems more understandable and
    interpretable by the impacted communities.
•   Pro-Equity and Anti-Racism: Any negative impact on vulnerable groups should be anticipated to the
    extent possible by the agency. If negative impacts occur, these impacts should be immediately
    addressed, and a plan should be in place to eliminate the cause of these negative impacts. Specifically,
    Washington State is mandated to address the exclusion, marginalization, and oppression of Black,
    Indigenous, People of Color, and other groups. The deeply entrenched inequities can be exacerbated by
    the usage of GenAI.
•   Generative AI images or video: Assess the impact of GenAI-created images, code, and verbiage on
    contracting, and address ingrained bias in the system causing skewed outputs. Images and videos can
    be sources of bias and misinformation, and therefore agencies need to be intentional in their use.
•   Generative AI usage transparency: Agencies should indicate when GenAI applications are being used
    in direct interactions with the public or when GenAI is used to produce, in part or in full, content, reports,
    images, or videos for public consumption. A simple disclaimer should be clearly posted on any
    generated content.
•   Data source accountability: Agencies should as much as possible seek out data sources that are
    ethically created and curated as they will be responsible for understanding and mitigating bias in their
    processes.
•   Bias detection and mitigation: Create a process to detect and address bias. Evaluate the reliability,
    quality, and representativeness of all data used in the system, and identify any potential biases,
    inequities, or other societal concerns related to the data used by the generative AI system.

•   Trust and reputation of government: It is important at all levels to foster public trust, especially with
    misinformation on AI, the lack of holistic controls, and the rapidly changing landscape. The amount of
    necessary mitigation can be scaled to the assessed risk level and use case.

Data
Primary guidelines for sourcing and privacy of data in GenAI systems should be adopted and deployed in
compliance with existing data protection policies and statutes. For equitable outcomes, data transparency
and responsible data sourcing are essential.

Data Sourcing and Transparency
    •   Identify data sources: Identify, to the extent possible, the underlying data and base models in an
        application.
    •   Identify data pathways: Agencies should understand what happens when a user submits data into
        a GenAI enabled system and understand how it might be used by the GenAI application.
    •   Hallucination tracking: A known phenomenon in AI where trained models will make connections to
        unrelated data, causing an output that is non-sensical. The frequency and root causes of these
        hallucinations in that data should be considered during the ongoing use of a GenAI application.
    •   Reliability: Agencies must be responsible for conclusions or deliverables that were generated and
        are accountable to the communities impacted by the use of AI. They should adopt or address in
        policy how reliability will be determined in GenAI outputs.

Data Guidelines for Enterprise Use and Implementation
    •   GenAI services inventory: The state enterprise should create and maintain a list of the GenAI
        applications. To support efficiency across agencies as well as build trust with community through
        transparency, information about these applications should be published without introducing
        security risks to the state.
    •   Opt-out options: GenAI Applications that are considered high-risk based on data concerns
        identified by the risk assessment: agencies should identify how the user can opt out of the AI
        components without impacting their access to public services. This includes instances where an
        individual is interacting with an AI or where an individual’s data might be used in an AI application.

Performance and Outputs
Please follow these guidelines to generate content, with the understanding that the GenAI system will only
provide outputs based on the data it is trained on. Agencies using GenAI should develop a plan to measure
performance based on their specific needs, especially for high-risk use cases, and consider whether the
outputs are meeting the State’s requirements. It is crucial to evaluate the effects on vulnerable and
underserved populations when assessing output performance of high-risk use cases and other use cases
where applicable.

Performance Metrics
    •   Measuring bias: Agencies should ensure that the system’s base model is checked against evidence
        of bias. If evidence of bias is found, agencies are expected to plan for either ending the use of the
        biased GenAI application or creating and executing a plan for mitigating these biases.
    •   Alignment with agency goals: Agencies should ensure their use of GenAI is measured in alignment
        with their intended business outcomes (for example, customer satisfaction, service quality, efficient
        service delivery, cost, etc.).
    •   High risk: Please refer to the executive order section 8, Risk Assessment.

    •   Evaluate the quality of inputs: Agencies should evaluate their GenAI input to ensure a precise
        prompt to receive a useful output. Evaluating inputs (prompts) is equally as important as evaluating
        outputs for bias and accuracy.

Performance Guidelines for Use and Implementation
    •   Explore all options: Agencies should consider if the GenAI application being used is the best way to
        get the needed results. Consider the pros and cons of using a particular system over other available
        systems or using a workforce or human-based approach.
    •   Consistency: Evaluate GenAI systems to ensure consistent performance across all uses. Individual
        outputs should be validated.
    •   Piloting: It is recommended to establish at least 6 months of a pilot plan for new applications with a
        significant number of unknowns, one that might have negative impacts on vulnerable groups.

Continuous Monitoring
Primary guidelines on how to structure a monitoring plan for a successful, transparent, and ongoing
implementation of GenAI use cases and their impacts on communities.

    •   Planning: Establish and implement a reasonable plan for regularly monitoring the GenAI system to
        ensure it operates as intended, based on the level of risk.
    •   Traceability: Document the results of monitoring activities and any corrective actions taken to
        ensure traceability and transparency.
    •   Opt-out: Specifically, for opt-out options for members of the public engaging with GenAI in their
        interactions with state government. Measure the results of the established opt-out option against
        the services provided with GenAI, in terms of timeliness, calculations, and outcomes.
    •   Public feedback: Identify a mechanism for public feedback scaled to risk level and use case,
        especially from groups at risk of negative impact.
    •   Transparency structure: Adopt or develop a transparency structure that keeps the public informed.
        If a problem is identified, impacted communities should be given direct notice and a collaborative
        plan should be created on how to mitigate these impacts. Agencies should post a simple disclaimer
        on any generated content.

Guiding Principles
The following guidance was adapted from the Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework created by
the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) within the U.S. Department of Commerce.
Equity: The use of GenAI should support the state’s work in delivering better and more equitable services and
outcomes to its residents. When directly or indirectly applicable, understand the benefits and burdens of the
use of GenAI for communities and mitigate the negative impacts on vulnerable or underserved groups. GenAI
technologies that optimize resource distribution can significantly benefit communities when applied
thoughtfully, ensuring that they contribute positively to societal goals.
Responsibility: As public stewards, agencies should use GenAI responsibly and be held accountable for the
performance, impact, and consequences of its use in agency work. AI-powered monitoring systems can
assist in improving operational efficiency, but they must be implemented with clear accountability structures
to manage their potential impacts.
Community: AI should not be used simply for the novelty of it. As such, AI applications should be utilized to
support and uplift communities, particularly those who are underserved and underrepresented. Fairness in
GenAI identifies concerns for equity by addressing issues such as harmful bias and discrimination. Systems
must be designed, implemented, and utilized in a way that avoids reinforcing or eliminates entirely existing
biases and focus on fairness, inclusion, and non-discrimination.
Transparency: Acting transparently and creating a record of GenAI processes can build trust and foster
collective learning. Transparency reflects the extent to which information about a GenAI system and its
outputs is available to the individuals interacting with the system. Transparency answers “what happened” in
the system. Automated systems that provide recommendations or decisions should be transparent about
how those outcomes are determined, allowing users to understand and trust the process.
Safety, Security, and Resiliency: GenAI should be used with safety and security in mind, minimizing
potential harm and ensuring that systems are reliable, resilient, and controllable by humans. AI systems
used by state agencies should not endanger human life, health, property, or the environment. For instance,
facial recognition technology can improve the efficiency of identity verification processes but also requires
careful monitoring to prevent privacy violations.
Reliability: Agencies are responsible for the ensuring the use of GenAI produces accurate and valid outputs
and demonstrates reliable system performance. In applications such as automated decision-making, AI can
help make processes more precise and improve efficiency. However, it’s essential to ensure these systems
and the humans that operate them avoid potential errors that could have significant consequences.
Privacy and Data Protection: GenAI should be used to respect user privacy, ensure data protection, and
comply with relevant privacy regulations and standards. Privacy values such as anonymity, confidentiality,
and control generally should guide choices for GenAI system design, development, and deployment. Privacy-
enhancing GenAI should safeguard human autonomy and identity where appropriate.
Explainable and Interpretable: Agencies should ensure GenAI use in the system can be explained, meaning
“how” the decisions made by the system can be understood. Interpretability of a system means an agency
can answer the “why” for a decision made by the system, and its meaning or context to the user. GenAI tools
that assess risk should be able to explain how they arrive at conclusions, helping the community to
understand and trust the output.
