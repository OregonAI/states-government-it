    1.    PURPOSE

          1.1.     This plan provides the basis of appropriate response to security incidents
                   that threaten or compromise the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of
                   State information assets, systems, and networks that deliver information.

    2.    SCOPE

          2.1.     This plan applies to all departments (including agencies, boards,
                   authorities, and commissions) within the Executive Branch of WV State
                   Government, excluding constitutional officers, the WV Board of Education,
                   the WV Department of Education, and the county boards of education that
                   have access to or use State-provided resources.

          2.2.     Where there are plans in place which provide less restrictions or security
                   than this policy, those plans will be superseded by this plan. In instances
                   where existing state and federal laws, regulations, policies, and plans are
                   more restrictive than this plan, the more restrictive provisions will prevail.

 3.   POLICY

      3.1.     Defining Security Incidents

             3.1.1.   A Security Incident is any event that involves misuse of computing
                      resources or is disruptive to normal system or data processing
                      operations. Examples include, but are not limited to the following:

                  3.1.1.1.    Loss of information confidentiality (data theft, accidental
                              disclosure);

                  3.1.1.2.    Compromise of information integrity (damage to data or
                              unauthorized modification or deletion);

                  3.1.1.3.    Theft of, or damage to, physical IT assets including
                              computers, storage devices, printers, etc;

                  3.1.1.4.    Denial of service (DoS) attack;

                  3.1.1.5.    Misuse of services, information, or assets;

                  3.1.1.6.    Overt threat of system interruption or failure;

                  3.1.1.7.    Loss of power to a critical system;

                  3.1.1.8.    Loss of connectivity to a critical system;

                  3.1.1.9.    Infection of systems by unauthorized or malicious software or
                              configurations;

                3.1.1.10.     Failed login attempts, exceeding the amount required to lock
                              an account;

                 3.1.1.11.    Unauthorized changes to State-owned hardware, software,
                              or configuration;

                3.1.1.12.     Reports of unusual system behavior; and

                3.1.1.13.     Intrusion detection alerts or alarms.

             3.1.2.   Security incidents range in severity from Non-Critical to Critical. The
                      categorization of an incident, according to its severity, will trigger the
                      appropriate response; therefore, triage and categorization of an
                      incident is critical to its proper management (Appendix A). For this
                      reason, triage and categorization should occur after it is reported,
                      by incident managers, not by reporters.

     3.2.     Defining Non-Critical Security Incidents

            3.2.1.   A non-critical security incident is one with either low or acceptable
                     risk to the State and its systems. Examples include, but are not
                     limited to the following:

                 3.2.1.1.    Employees accessing prohibited websites that do not
                             jeopardize the State’s network;

                 3.2.1.2.    Violating applicable information security policies and/or
                             procedures deemed not to involve critical operations or
                             impact;

                 3.2.1.3.    Any security incident that is restricted to a single non-critical
                             computer or system; and

                 3.2.1.4.    Common system failure with routine recovery as standard
                             operating procedure.

     3.3.     Defining Critical Security Incidents

            3.3.1.   A critical security incident is one with high impact and high threat,
                     which can result in both high risk and great vulnerability to the State
                     and its systems. Examples include, but are not limited to the
                     following:

                 3.3.1.1.    Loss of system functionality;

                 3.3.1.2.    Theft of confidential and/or sensitive information;

                 3.3.1.3.    Interruption of network services;

                 3.3.1.4.    Disruption or denial of service of critical systems (ex: clinical
                             decision-support applications, financial reporting systems,
                             mailing systems and electronic medical records information);

                 3.3.1.5.    Theft of a device with legally protected information,

                 3.3.1.6.    Unauthorized access to security administrator applications or
                             information; and

                 3.3.1.7.    Certain policy or procedure violations.

            3.3.2.   Critical security incidents require that a Computer Security Incident
                     Response Team (CSIRT) be assembled to apply appropriate
                     response.

            3.3.3.   A security incident is deemed critical if it meets one of the following
                     conditions.

                 3.3.3.1.    An appropriate member of the CSIRT declares it to be
                             critical;

                 3.3.3.2.    The Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) declares it to
                             be critical;

                 3.3.3.3.    The Chief Information Officer (CIO) declares it to be critical;

                 3.3.3.4.    The Security Incident Advisory Board (SIAB) declares it to be
                             critical.

     3.4.     Defining Incident Severity Levels

            3.4.1.   Information security incident severity levels are based upon all
                     available information about system and data criticality (business
                     impact), sensitivity, and other variables, such as resource
                     requirements and the ability to restore/recover to normal
                     functioning. Categories are as follows:

                 3.4.1.1.    Category 3: Example: Small numbers of system probes or
                             scans detected on internal and/or external systems; isolated
                             instances of malware easily mitigated by endpoint security
                             software; intelligence received concerning threats to which
                             systems may be vulnerable.

                 3.4.1.2.    Category 2: Example: Significant numbers of system probes
                             or scans detected; Denial of Service attacks attempted with
                             no impact on operations; widespread instances of malware
                             easily mitigated by endpoint security software; isolated instances
                             of a new kind of malware not handled by endpoint security
                             software.

                 3.4.1.3.    Category 1: Example: Successful penetration or Denial of
                             Service attacks, with limited or significant impact on
                             operations; incidents involving sensitive information such as
                             federal tax information (FTI), protected health information
                             (PHI), personally identifiable information (PII), or personal
                             financial information (PFI); widespread instances of a new
                             malware not handled by endpoint security software;
                             significant risk of negative financial or public relations impact;
                             natural disasters, major outages, building shutdown; system
                             failures.

              3.5.3.6.    Establish controls for the proper collection and preservation
                          of evidence, if required;

              3.5.3.7.    Conduct a post-incident critique;

              3.5.3.8.    Revise responses as needed to prevent further intrusions;
                          and

              3.5.3.9.    Document all actions and results.

        3.7.3.    Incident Response Phase

              3.7.3.1.    During this phase, CSIRT members will perform the following
                          tasks:

                   3.7.3.1.1.    Gather and analyze evidence (log files,audit trails,
                                 etc.) to determine (1) how to stop the incident; (2) the
                                 cause of the incident; (3) the vulnerability or
                                 vulnerabilities being exploited; (4) how to eliminate
                                 vulnerabilities; and (5) additional mitigation activities.

                   3.7.3.1.2.    Make an assessment of how far the incident has
                                 spread, i.e., which systems are involved, and the
                                 extent that have been compromised.

                   3.7.3.1.3.    If a privacy violation has occurred, appropriate
                                 response to breach law, including notifications, should
                                 be completed.

        3.7.4.    Incident Recovery Phase

 4.   DEFINITIONS

      4.1.   Availability – Having reliable and timely access to the data and resources
             you are authorized to use.

      4.2.   Chief Information Security Officer (CISO) – Person designated by the CIO
             to oversee Information Security practices and initiatives for the Executive
             Branch of WV State government, excluding the constitutional officers.

      4.3.   Chief Information Officer (CIO) – The person responsible for the State’s
             information resources.

                                     Page 13 of 18

                       Incident Management Plan

     4.4.   Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) – A group of
            individuals who have been trained in computer security incident
            management, each having distinct response roles, and a familiarity with
            the fundamentals of gathering and handling evidence. Members of this
            team must be adequately skilled, possess general expertise about various
            information systems, be on call twenty-four hours a day, and be capable of
            responding within a nominal amount of time.

     4.5.   Computer Incident Response Team (CSIRT) Manager – Designated leader
            of the CSIRT, who has direct communication with upper management
            during a security incident response. They will coordinate and dispense
            critical information to both upper and middle management.

     4.6.   Confidentiality – The concept of keeping private information away from
            individuals who should not have access.

     4.7.   Contractor – Anyone who has a contract with the State or a state entity..

     4.8.   Denial of Service (DoS) Attack – attack that prevents or impairs authorized
            use of networks, systems, or applications by overloading resources.

     4.9.   Information Assets – Any of the data, hardware, software, network,
            documentation, and personnel used to manage and process information.

    4.10.   Information Security incident - Any known successful or unsuccessful
            attempt by an authorized or unauthorized individual to inappropriately use,
            disclose, modify, access, or destroy any electronic information.

    4.11.   Integrity – maintains that data is consistent and hasn’t been modified.

    4.12.   West Virginia Cybersecurity Office (CSO) - The functional unit charged
            with the responsibility to undertake and sustain initiatives to promote,
            enhance, monitor, and govern actions, standards, and activities necessary
            to safeguard data and information systems within the Executive Branch of
            WV, as provided in West Virginia Code §5A-6-4a and the Governor's
            Executive Order No. 6-06.

    4.13.   Personally Identifiable Information (PII) –Includes all protected and
            non-protected information that identifies, or can be used to identify, locate,
            or contact an individual.

    4.14.   Privacy Incident– A breach in which there is knowledge or reasonable
            belief that there has been, or may have been, unauthorized or
            inappropriate collection, use, access, disclosure, modification, and/or
            exposure of PHI or PII.

      4.15.   Protected Health Information (PHI) – Health information transmitted by or
              maintained in electronic media used to identify an individual, which is
              created, used, or disclosed in the course of providing health care services
              such as diagnosis or treatment. Examples include: names, phone
              numbers, medical record numbers, photos, etc.

      4.16.   Security Incident Advisory Board (SIAB) - This board is comprised of
              senior IT staff and other mid-to-high level managers throughout various
              State agencies, as well as ad-hoc, incident-specific, members. This group
              is responsible for making response decisions and budget requests above
              the level delegated to the CSIRT Manager.

      4.17.   Security Operations Center (SOC) – The SOC is part of WVOT’s
              Information Security Team that focuses on monitoring activities,

APPENDIX A: Computer Security Incident Response Team(CSIRT) Duties
The CSIRT Manager will be responsible for the following duties:
• Assist in the development and promotion of security policy and procedures, such as
password and intrusion detection policies, as well as backup and recovery
procedures.

• Select and train Computer Security Incident Response Team (CSIRT) members and
coordinators, as well as standing Advisory Board members.

• Develop a representative inventory of critical security incidents, based upon historical
incident events.

• Maintain information security incident response handling procedures regarding
notification, assessment, investigation, remediation, monitoring, and reporting to CISO.

• Maintain procedures or protocols to report criminal-related security incidents to
outside authorities complying with appropriate legal requirements and regulations,
including who the reporting official should be under differing circumstances, and to
whom the official should report the various incident types.

• Inform and coordinate security incident response activities within the CSIRT.

• Maintain documentation related to security incidents, such as, how it occurred, where
the attack came from, the response, and whether the response was effective.

• Preserve evidence, i.e., keep lists of witnesses, maintain copies of logs, e-mail, and
other communication; maintain a chain of custody of evidence.

• Take action to prevent further intrusion or damage and remove the cause of the
problem.

• Determine source of intrusion and take steps to prevent an immediate re-infection.

• Coordinate CSIRT’s restoration of affected systems to their original state and
preserve evidence by backing up logs or possibly the entire system while minimizing
business interruptions.

• Assess the damage to the State and estimate both the damage cost and the cost of
the containment efforts.

• Conduct a post-incident evaluation of the incident characteristics, and the incident
response activities, and revise procedures, response planning, and policies, as
needed to reduce the risk of future incidents, and improve the response
methodology.
